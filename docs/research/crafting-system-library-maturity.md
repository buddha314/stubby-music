# Crafting libraries thrive only inside engines

*Research, 2026-11-01. Sourced notes: `crafting-system-library-maturity/`. Acted on in `add-recipes-bevy-plugin`.*

*Research report, 2026-11-01. Sources: the notes in `research_notes/Crafting system library maturity/` (engine plugins, language libraries, publishing precedents), plus the earlier `docs/research/crafting-and-inventory-crates.md` and `docs/research/crafting-and-goal-tracking.md`. Registry figures were read from crates.io, npm, NuGet, PyPI and GitHub on 2026-11-01. Where a statement is my reasoning rather than a sourced fact, it is marked* **(inference)**.

Crafting, inventory and recipe systems are mature **only when they are tied to an engine or to one game's data ecosystem**. Unity has a paid market led by Opsive's Ultimate Inventory System. Godot has two MIT add-ons with roughly 700 to 1,000 GitHub stars each. Unreal has Epic's Lyra fragment pattern and a set of survival templates. No ecosystem in any language has a widely adopted, engine-agnostic crafting library. In Rust, the biggest game-inventory crates have about 10,000 to 13,000 lifetime downloads and are dormant. Nothing on crates.io combines data-driven recipes, a persistent recipe book, resource nodes with replenishment and a production-rate solver. So the niche is real and empty. It is also littered with small abandoned attempts, which says the hard part is demand and design fit, not code. My recommendation for `stubby_recipes` is **not yet**. Now, cut a domain-neutral core *inside the game repo*: move the waveform and signal-use types out of it, keep the rules toolchain, and add goals. Publish to crates.io as `MIT OR Apache-2.0`, at `0.1.0`, from its own repo with an optional Bevy adapter, once four conditions hold. First, the core carries no stubby-specific types. Second, the goals reducer and at least one real production recipe have shipped in the game. Third, a non-Bevy example runs. Fourth, the public API has survived about two months of game development without a breaking change. Until then, publishing adds a release step to every cross-cutting change and buys an audience nobody has shown exists.

## Every mature system is bound to an engine or one game

**Unity has the deepest market and the most churn.** Opsive's Ultimate Inventory System is the clearest maintained framework. It costs $90 per seat, has 73 reviews, and v1.3.10 shipped on Sep 12, 2026, supporting Unity 2022.1 through 6000.6 ([Asset Store](https://assetstore.unity.com/packages/tools/game-toolkits/ultimate-inventory-system-166053)). Its architecture is the one to study. Data (item categories, definitions and attributes with inheritance; recipes) is kept separate from rules ("Crafting Processors") and from a UI that "only presents and binds". Ingredients can be a definition, a whole category (interchangeable substitutes) or a specific item instance. **It has no built-in timed crafting**: delayed completion, station state and ingredient quality all go into custom processors ([Opsive crafting docs](https://opsive.com/support/documentation/ultimate-inventory-system/crafting/)). Reviews range from "very stable and highly capable" to "multiple ways this asset can easily break", and one reviewer called it "a flexible, modular framework rather than a plug-and-play solution" ([reviews](https://assetstore.unity.com/packages/tools/game-toolkits/ultimate-inventory-system-166053/reviews)). Around it is a trail of exits. Devdog's Inventory Pro sold more than 100,000 licences, then was released under MIT and left "entirely in the hands of the community" ([GitHub](https://github.com/devdogio/Inventory-Pro)), because support cost more than it earned ([GameFromScratch](https://gamefromscratch.com/?p=11047)). "Ultimate Inventory & Crafting 5 PRO" and the "Inventory System (Full/Lite)" pair are deprecated ([Asset Store](https://assetstore.unity.com/packages/tools/gui/ultimate-inventory-crafting-5-pro-19900)).

**Unreal ships a pattern, not a crafting system.** Lyra defines an item as a data-only definition composed of "extensible inventory fragments", with equipment kept separate ([Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/lyra-inventory-and-equipment-in-unreal-engine)). Arc Inventory refines this with static fragments on a definition and dynamic fragments on each stack ([Arc docs](https://docs.punyhuman.com/arcinventory/concepts/itemstack/)). Crafting usually arrives as a bolt-on or inside a survival template. Games by Hyper's world stations put finished items into their own inventory component, which the player then collects ([Hyper docs](https://gamesbyhyper.com/docs/project-foundations/template-logic-and-flexibility/)), a shape that maps directly onto factory machines. Easy Survival RPG V5 advertises queue-based crafting ([Fab](https://www.fab.com/listings/bc8e1ee8-5b82-40df-ad2c-226524ca6253)). Several older Marketplace crafting plugins were never migrated to Fab ([UE Marketplace](https://www.unrealengine.com/marketplace/zh-CN/product/survival-rpg-inventory-equipment-system/reviews)).

**Godot's add-ons are leaner and open.** GLoot has 990 stars and an MIT licence. It provides stack items driven by JSON prototypes, constraint objects (grid, weight, count) and dictionary serialization, with UI controls kept separate, but **no crafting** ([GitHub](https://github.com/peter-kish/gloot)). expressobits' Inventory System (739 stars, MIT, C++ GDExtension core) has CraftStation nodes. Its recipes separate **consumed ingredients from required-but-not-consumed tools**, such as a hammer ([expressobits docs](https://expressobits.com/inventory-system/getting_started/introduction.html)). Pandora (about 1.1k stars) describes itself as "STILL IN ALPHA AND NOT PRODUCTION-READY" ([GitHub](https://github.com/bitbrain/pandora)).

Every serious system across the three engines shares a common feature set: definitions separate from instances, categories or tags, stacking, typed attributes, recipes as data, serialization, and logic separate from UI. The features that set them apart are network replication, category substitution, catalysts, stations that own an output inventory, and queued crafting. Two observations matter most for stubby-music. Even the market leader leaves time and stations to custom code. And **no surveyed system models continuous, throughput-based factory production**, meaning machines with rates feeding one another; they all model discrete crafts that the player starts **(inference** from the feature lists above**)**.

## Outside engines, the niche is empty and full of abandoned attempts

Across languages the pattern holds. Maturity comes from coupling to a host, and every standalone generic attempt has tiny usage.

| Ecosystem | Largest generic crafting/inventory library | Usage | State |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rust | `inv-sys` / `game_inventory` | 12,984 / 10,477 lifetime downloads | last release 2021 / 2024 ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/inv-sys), [crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/game_inventory)) |
| Rust (2026 wave) | `game-utils-inventory`, `bevy_inventory`, `openstranded-common-crafting` | 81, 201, 65 downloads | single-author first releases ([lib.rs](https://lib.rs/crates/game-utils-inventory), [lib.rs](https://lib.rs/crates/bevy_inventory), [crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/openstranded-common-crafting/0.1.0)) |
| C#/.NET | `GameLib` 0.1.5 | 33,786 downloads | the largest generic hit ([NuGet](https://www.nuget.org/packages/GameLib)) |
| C#/.NET (engine-agnostic) | `InventoryFramework.SDK` | 2,395 downloads | commercial gRPC client to a server ([NuGet](https://www.nuget.org/packages/inventoryframework.sdk)) |
| npm | `craftingrecipes`, `voxel-recipes` | about 19 weekly | voxel.js-era, abandoned ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/voxel-recipes)) |
| npm (game-bound) | `prismarine-recipe` | about 62,550 weekly | Minecraft-only ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/prismarine-recipe)) |
| GitHub topics | `topic:crafting-system` | 12 repos in total, top has 3 stars | ([GitHub](https://github.com/topics/crafting-system)) |

Rust specifically has **no recipe-graph engine, no production planner and no progression or unlock crate**. Searches for "tech tree", "recipe book" and "production planner" returned nothing relevant ([crates.io search](https://crates.io/search?q=tech%20tree)). The only Rust "recipe calculator", `gac`, is Factorio-specific and has been dormant since 2023 ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/gac)). The 2026 cluster of new single-author crates shows rising interest but no adoption. `bevy_inventory` calls itself a pure core yet turns on `bevy` 0.18 as a *default* feature, and it has no recipes ([lib.rs](https://lib.rs/crates/bevy_inventory)).

Factory planners point to the most interesting gap. FactorioLab (881 stars) runs **14 games on one calculator engine**, including Satisfactory, Dyson Sphere Program and Captain of Industry, using per-game datasets with a schema ([GitHub](https://github.com/factoriolab/factoriolab)). Its solver is GLPK, compiled for TypeScript ([package.json](https://github.com/factoriolab/factoriolab/blob/main/package.json)). Kirk McDonald's calculator embeds its own exact-rational simplex ([GitHub](https://github.com/KirkMcDonald/kirkmcdonald.github.io)). The rate math is a solved LP problem. What nobody ships as a library is the domain layer above the solver: items, recipes, machines, rates and multi-output handling. The one npm attempt to extract a Factorio calculator core, `@kgrech/factorio-calculator-core`, has 0 weekly downloads and was last published in 2021 ([npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@kgrech/factorio-calculator-core)). FactorioLab's 14 games are the strongest evidence that a game-agnostic **recipe-graph model** works. That evidence is for an app's internal model, though, not for a library people depend on.

There is **no cross-game interchange format** either. The formats that took hold are per-ecosystem. One is PrismarineJS minecraft-data, a cross-*language* standard for one game ([GitHub](https://github.com/PrismarineJS/minecraft-data)). Another is JEI's plugin API, which EMI loads through a compatibility layer ([JEI](https://github.com/mezz/JustEnoughItems); [EMI](https://modrinth.com:443/mod/emi)). Both grew from one huge modding ecosystem with many independent producers and consumers of recipe data **(inference)**.

**Why dialogue and ECS libraries escaped their engines and crafting did not (inference).** Yarn Spinner's core is "engine-agnostic" C# with Unity, Godot and Unreal integrations ([GitHub](https://github.com/YarnSpinnerTool/YarnSpinner)). ink is MIT, has an editor (Inky) and a JSON story format, and came out of inkle's own shipped games ([GitHub](https://github.com/inkle/ink)). EnTT has 13,237 stars ([GitHub](https://github.com/skypjack/entt)). These succeeded because their domain is uniform across games, because they give authors something to write in, and because a shipped game stands behind them. Crafting rules vary enormously (grid or shapeless recipes, quality, timers, stations, rates, unlocks, spoilage). A generic library therefore tends to be either too thin to matter, since a recipe is a struct plus a JSON file, or too opinionated to fit. This is why I read the many dead attempts as a weak-demand signal, not as an invitation.

## What in stubby_recipes is generic and what is stubby's

`stubby_recipes` is about 1,680 lines with no Bevy or audio dependency (`crates/stubby_recipes/src/{lib,rules,source}.rs`, `bin/rules_fmt.rs`). Two crates consume it today: `stubby_game` and the repo's `jackdaw/` crate. Its unusual strength is not crafting. It is the **rules toolchain**: strict per-domain JSON with `deny_unknown_fields`, structured `file [id] .field: message` errors reported all at once, snake_case IDs unique across files, reachability checks, an append-only `ids.lock`, a migrations table that treats IDs as save-game API, canonical formatting, a `"format": 1` version field, and JSON Schemas generated with `schemars`. That covers almost every failure mode the earlier research catalogued in shipped games: Factorio's silent unknown fields, Minecraft's progress lost on rename, invisible unregistered content. Compared with the publishing notes, it also matches what made ink and LDtk durable: **a documented, versioned data format with a published schema** ([LDtk JSON](https://ldtk.io/docs/game-dev/json-overview/)) **(inference)**. Nothing surveyed in any ecosystem packages this "rules as validated data rows" layer as a library.

The model itself is partly generic and partly stubby's own. The split below comes from reading the code against the survey **(inference)**.

| Concept | Today | Generic? | In the published core |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Items, `Stack`, `Inventory` (map, u32, overflow-checked) | `lib.rs` | Yes | Yes, with an `extensions`/metadata hook |
| `Waveform`, `SignalUse` (`vco`/`lfo`/`gate`) on `ItemDefinition` | `lib.rs` | **No** | Stays in the game |
| Recipes with atomic `craft`, `missing_ingredients` | `lib.rs` | Yes | Yes |
| Exhaustible resources, `HarvestLedger` keyed by instance ID | `lib.rs` | Yes | Yes |
| `ReplenishmentPolicy::{Never, After, NextNight}` | `lib.rs` | Mostly | Yes, with "night" generalised to a named calendar event the host supplies |
| `ResourceTime { elapsed_millis, nights_started }` | `lib.rs` | Shape is game-specific | Becomes host-provided clock/event counters |
| Mines producing a *resource* (not items) | `source.rs` | Yes, and uncommon | Yes |
| `ProductivityFactor::{WaterQuality, LightQuality}` | `source.rs` | **No** (the names) | Opaque string or generic factor IDs; the game's names stay in data |
| Recipe book that persists at zero | `lib.rs` | Yes (Hiro's `keep_zero` is the same idea, [Hiro](https://heroiclabs.com/docs/hiro/concepts/inventory/llm.md)) | Yes |
| Rules loader, validator, ID lock, migrations, `rules-fmt`, schemas | `rules.rs`, bin | Yes, and the differentiator | Yes, as the headline feature |
| `shipped_files()` embedding `assets/recipes/` | `rules.rs` | **No** | Stays in the game |
| Goals (long/short horizon, partial delivery, `after`, pins, pure `apply` reducer) | spec only (`add-inventory-and-goals-hud`) | Yes; matches Satisfactory milestones | After it ships in the game, not before |
| Machines, timed production, rates, LP planner | not written | Yes, and the biggest open gap | Not in 0.1; a later, separate decision |

Two design rules come out of this split. First, game metadata should travel as **data, not types**. The cleanest form is an opaque, round-tripped `extensions` object on item, resource, mine and recipe definitions. It would be validated by a schema fragment the game supplies, and it suits JSON Schema's `additionalProperties`. The alternative is a generic `Item<M>` parameter, which is zero-cost and type-safe but spreads a type parameter through every API and makes the generated schemas per-game **(inference**, building on the options in the publishing notes**)**. The lightweight choice is extensions, plus a game-side newtype that parses `waveform` and `uses` out of them. Second, the core should stop naming things the game invented: night, water quality, light quality and `square_wave`. Today those names are compiled into enums.

## How to publish it, if and when the conditions hold

**Structure.** Copy the rapier pattern, not Yarn's. Dimforge keeps the engine-free core and the Bevy plugin in one repo, with the plugin under `bindings/` ([rapier](https://github.com/dimforge/rapier)). It versions them independently: `bevy_rapier3d` 0.37 sits on `rapier3d` 0.36 and was released two days after Bevy 0.20 ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/bevy_rapier3d)). By contrast, `yarnspinner_core` has an optional `bevy ^0.19` feature, so even its "core" names a Bevy version ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/yarnspinner_core)). Twenty-four days after Bevy 0.20 it still has no 0.20 release ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/bevy_yarnspinner)). Bevy breaks roughly every 3 to 5 months: 0.18, 0.19 and 0.20 landed in January, June and October 2026 ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/bevy)). The suggested repo is one Cargo workspace:

```
<name>/                 core: model, transactions, rules loader, validator, migrations, schemas
                        deps: serde, serde_json, thiserror, schemars (feature), serde_path_to_error
<name>-cli/             the rules-fmt binary, generalised (format, --check, schema, lock)
bevy_<name>/            optional adapter: asset loader, messages, Reflect wrappers; bevy default-features = false
examples/cli_catalog/   a non-Bevy example (required; see conditions)
schema/                 generated JSON Schemas, checked in CI
```

**The adapter can wait.** The game already keeps its Bevy glue in `stubby_game` and plans to use `bevy_common_assets` for loading ([earlier survey](https://github.com/NiklasEi/bevy_common_assets)). A first release can therefore be **core plus CLI only**, which keeps it off the Bevy release treadmill entirely. The LDtk precedent cuts the other way, though. `bevy_ecs_ldtk` kept its users while the standalone parsers `ldtk_rust` and `ldtk2` went stale ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/ldtk_rust), [crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/ldtk2)). A core with only Bevy users effectively becomes part of an adapter. Ship `bevy_<name>` once there is a second Bevy user who asks for it, and not before **(inference)**.

**Naming, licence, versions.** Bevy recommends `MIT OR Apache-2.0` for compatibility and asks plugin authors to ask before claiming generic `bevy_` names ([Bevy plugin guide](https://bevy.org/learn/quick-start/plugin-development/)). The game repo is plain MIT, so dual-licensing an extracted crate requires the copyright holder to agree, which is the designer here. Avoid "stubby" in the name, since the point is to be domain-neutral. Avoid the bare generic name `crafting` too. It was unclaimed on 2026-11-01, but taking it would promise a general crafting system the crate does not aim to be **(inference)**. Names that describe the actual differentiator, all unclaimed on crates.io as of 2026-11-01 by API lookup, are `rulebook`, `craftbook` and `recipe_book`. `rulebook` fits best, because the strongest feature is validated rules data, not crafting UX. Start at **0.1.0** and treat the 0.x minor as the breaking digit, per Cargo's SemVer rules ([Cargo SemVer](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/semver.html)). Mark public enums such as `RecipeError` and `ReplenishmentPolicy` `#[non_exhaustive]` and make struct fields private before 0.1, so additions do not use up minors. Today's `pub` fields on every definition struct would make any new field a breaking change. Declare `rust-version`, name features `serde`/`schemars` with `dep:` syntax, and fill in Cargo metadata ([API guidelines](https://rust-lang.github.io/api-guidelines/checklist.html)). Version the **data format** separately from the crate. Keep the existing `"format": 1` field, and document that a crate minor may only bump it together with a migration. This is the LDtk `jsonVersion` discipline.

**What 0.1 contains and leaves out.** It contains items and stacks with extensions, inventory, recipes with atomic craft, exhaustible resources with the harvest ledger, replenishment policies over host-named calendar events, mines that produce resources, the recipe book, the goals reducer (only once it has shipped in the game), and the whole rules toolchain. It does not contain UI, ECS types, slots or grids, weight, networking, a save system beyond serde, machines or timers, rates, or an LP planner. All of these belong in a README "Non-goals" section, in the spirit of hecs's "a library, not a framework" ([hecs](https://github.com/Ralith/hecs)). Category ingredients (Opsive) and catalysts (expressobits) are cheap additions for later, and each should arrive as an OpenSpec rule-type change in the game first.

**Keeping the game in sync.** Extract with `git filter-repo --subdirectory-filter crates/stubby_recipes` on a fresh clone to keep its history ([git-filter-repo](https://www.mankier.com/1/git-filter-repo)). In the game, depend on the published version. While co-developing, override it with `[patch.crates-io] rulebook = { path = "../rulebook" }`, placed in an **uncommitted `.cargo/config.toml`**, because `[patch]` is honoured only at the workspace root ([Cargo book](https://doc.rust-lang.org/cargo/reference/overriding-dependencies.html)). That fits this repo's worktree-per-agent rule. Each worktree can point at its own checkout of the library without touching the shared `Cargo.toml`. Keep a thin `stubby_recipes` crate in the game, holding `Waveform`, `SignalUse`, the embedded `assets/recipes/` files, and the parsing of the game's extensions. Then `stubby_game` and `jackdaw/` change only their import paths. A scheduled CI job in the game can build against the library's `main` to catch drift early, while releases stay pinned **(inference)**. inkjs shows what happens without this: a separately maintained port running "usually one major version behind" ([ink](https://github.com/inkle/ink)).

## The cost is a release step on every change, for unproven demand

The risks are concrete. **Demand is unproven.** The evidence is entirely negative space: an empty niche alongside a dozen near-zero-download attempts in every registry. No usage survey says how many teams would adopt rather than roll their own. **Maintenance moves to the critical path.** Today a rules change is one PR. After extraction it is a library PR, a release, a version bump and a game PR. Under this repo's split ownership, where Claude handles art, Codex handles crates, and several agents work at once, that turns ordinary game work into cross-repo coordination **(inference)**. **The API is not settled.** `recipes.json` is empty, goals are specified but not built, and machines, timed production and fluids (`add-fluid-quality-and-viscosity`) will push on the transaction model. Publishing now would spend 0.x minors on churn the game itself causes. **Leakage cuts both ways.** Publishing too early freezes stubby's waveform types into a "generic" crate. Over-generalising, on the other hand, makes the game pay for flexibility it does not need.

The case *for* is also real. The rules toolchain is the one piece no ecosystem offers. It is small. And the separation work (extensions instead of enums, host-named calendar events) improves the game's own architecture whether or not anything is ever published **(inference)**. That is why the recommendation is **not yet, but start the boundary now**:

| Step | When | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Move `Waveform`, `SignalUse`, productivity-factor names and `shipped_files` into a game-side layer; add `extensions`; make fields private and enums `#[non_exhaustive]` | Now, as an OpenSpec change in the game repo | Low; it is good hygiene regardless |
| Implement the goals `apply` reducer in the core, and ship at least one real production recipe | With `add-inventory-and-goals-hud` | Already planned |
| Add a non-Bevy example (load rules, harvest, craft, deliver to a goal) | After the reducer lands | Small |
| **Go** to its own repo and crates.io 0.1.0 (core + CLI, no adapter) | When all four conditions in the summary hold, *and* someone outside the project has asked for it, or the designer wants it public as a portfolio piece and accepts the release step | One-off extraction plus ongoing releases |
| `bevy_<name>` adapter; machines/rates/planner | Only on demand | Bevy treadmill, 3 to 4 releases a year |

## Conclusion

The survey changes the framing of the question. A standalone *crafting* library has failed to find users in every ecosystem, because crafting is where games differ. A standalone *rules-data* library has no competitor: strict, versioned item, recipe and source data with migrations, an ID lock and generated schemas, which is the thing every shipped systemic game builds in-house and lenient loaders get wrong. If `stubby_recipes` is ever published, it should be pitched as that, with crafting, harvesting and goals as the reference domain on top, rather than as another inventory crate. The second underserved opportunity, a production-rate model and planner above a generic LP solver of the kind FactorioLab proves out across 14 games, is the one most likely to draw outside users. It also matches what stubby-music has to build next anyway. That makes "publish once machines and rates exist in the game" a more promising trigger than "publish what we have now" **(inference)**.
